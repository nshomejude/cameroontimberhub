When a household buys a table or a contractor buys roof timber, the wood usually arrives already finished — planed, maybe already built into the piece itself — and the steps before that point are invisible. Understanding those steps is not just background trivia. Knowing what happens between forest and furniture is what lets a buyer ask sensible questions and recognise a piece that has been handled well.

## Harvest: where the wood begins

Legally harvested timber in Cameroon comes out of an area under some form of management — a logging concession, a council forest, a community forest, or a sale of standing volume, each licensed and inspected under Cameroon's forestry law and overseen by [MINFOF](https://www.minfof.cm/), the forestry and wildlife ministry. Trees are selected and felled, generally on a cutting cycle intended to let the area regenerate rather than being cleared outright. The logs are then extracted and moved out of the forest, historically the stage where poor practice does the most environmental damage — badly planned extraction tracks cause more erosion and collateral tree loss than the felling itself.

This is also the stage where legality is decided. A log without a documented origin at this point never gets a clean origin later; every downstream buyer is trusting whatever the first link in the chain actually did.

## Sawmilling: the log becomes usable material

Logs are converted into sawn timber — boards and beams cut to rough dimensions — either at an industrial sawmill or, for much of the domestic supply that never leaves the country, at smaller local mills closer to where the timber will be used. This is where a log's characteristics stop being abstract and start mattering practically: how it is sawn affects how much usable timber comes out of it, how the grain runs in the resulting boards, and how the timber will behave as it dries.

Species matters here too. A dense, durable species like [Azobe](species:azobe) is typically sawn for structural and heavy-duty use — beams, decking, ground-contact work — while a lighter, easier-to-work species like [Ayous](species:ayous) is sawn with furniture-grade and interior joinery in mind. [Sapele](species:sapele) and [Iroko](species:iroko) sit in between, common for both furniture and general construction depending on the cut.

## Drying: the step most often skipped

Freshly sawn timber holds a lot of moisture and will shrink, warp or crack as that moisture leaves — which is exactly what happens if it is used or finished too soon. Proper practice is to dry the timber, either by stacking it with spacers to air-dry over time or by kiln-drying it faster under controlled conditions, until it reaches a moisture content suited to its end use.

This is the step that gets rushed under price and time pressure, and it is also the step a buyer can actually judge for themselves. Timber that has not dried enough tends to feel unusually heavy for its size, and furniture made from it is the kind that develops gaps, warps, or cracks a few months after purchase — often well after the buyer has forgotten to connect the fault back to the wood rather than the workmanship. [How to verify claims when buying furniture](insights:how-to-verify-your-furniture-cameroon) covers practical checks for this.

## Processing and joinery: where a carpenter's skill shows

From dried, sawn timber, the material moves into secondary processing — planing to final dimension, cutting joints, shaping, and assembly. For domestic furniture this is very often the work of an individual carpenter or a small workshop rather than an industrial factory, which is part of what makes the domestic market feel different from buying imported, factory-made pieces: there is a person who selected, cut and joined that specific piece, and who can usually explain their choices if asked.

Joint quality, wood movement allowance (leaving room for timber to expand and contract with humidity) and finish all get decided here, and all affect how long a piece lasts far more than the species alone does.

## Retail: where the chain reaches the buyer

The finished product reaches a buyer either directly from a carpenter or workshop, through a furniture retailer, or — for raw timber rather than furniture — through a timber yard selling sawn and dried stock by the piece. This is the only part of the chain most buyers ever see, which is exactly why it helps to know what came before it. A seller who can speak plainly about where their timber was harvested, how long it dried, and who built the piece is showing you the whole chain, not just the last link in it.
