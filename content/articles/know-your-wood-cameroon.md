Walking into a timber yard or discussing options with a carpenter in Cameroon usually means confronting a handful of species names without much guidance on what separates them. This is a working primer for a domestic buyer — not a full technical species database, but a plain answer to "what is this wood usually used for, and why."

For full technical detail on any of these — density figures, working properties, durability class — see [the species database](species:); this article is about matching a species to an everyday domestic job.

## Furniture-grade species

**[Ayous](species:ayous)** is one of the most widely used woods in Cameroonian domestic furniture and joinery. It is light, pale, easy to cut and shape, and takes finishing well, which makes it a common choice for cabinetry, panelling and furniture where weight and cost matter more than maximum durability. It is not the wood to choose for something that will sit outdoors or take heavy structural load.

**[Sapele](species:sapele)** is a step up in weight and durability, with a reddish-brown colour and a distinctive interlocked grain that often shows a ribbon-like figure when finished. It is a common choice for furniture, doors and joinery where appearance matters as much as function, and it holds up to more everyday wear than Ayous.

**[Padouk](species:padouk)** stands out immediately for colour — a vivid orange-red that darkens with age and light exposure. It is used domestically where that colour is the point: decorative furniture pieces, accents, and work where a buyer specifically wants a striking, distinctive appearance rather than a neutral tone.

## Structural and heavy-duty species

**[Azobe](species:azobe)** is dense, heavy and highly durable, including in conditions that would rot a lighter wood — ground contact, exposure to moisture. It is the species to reach for on structural work: heavy beams, decking, and construction elements that need to bear load and survive weather over years, not the species to choose for a lightweight household item.

**[Iroko](species:iroko)** sits between the furniture-grade and structural categories. It is durable and stable enough for outdoor and semi-structural use — window frames, doors, some furniture — while still being workable enough for joinery, which is why it turns up across both construction and furniture use in practice.

**[Moabi](species:moabi)** is a dense, durable species used both structurally and decoratively — it has been used for flooring and heavy furniture as well as construction elements, valued for a combination of durability and a workable, attractive grain.

## Matching a species to your job, in short

The consistent pattern across all of these is that density and durability trade off against weight and ease of working. A species chosen for a light, easily shaped piece of furniture — Ayous is the clearest example — is not the species to choose for a load-bearing beam, and a dense structural species is usually more wood, more cost, and more effort than a decorative item needs. [Choosing timber for construction or furniture](insights:choosing-timber-construction-furniture-cameroon) goes further into matching species and grade to a specific project. If you are buying a finished piece rather than raw timber, ask your carpenter which species they used and why — a straight, specific answer ("Sapele, because it holds up to daily use and matches the rest of the room") is a good sign in itself, independent of which species they name.
