Choosing timber is not just choosing a species — it is matching a species, a grade and a piece of material to what the job actually demands. This is a practical framework for that decision, building on [Know your wood](insights:know-your-wood-cameroon) for species detail.

## Start with the demand the job places on the wood, not the species name

Before naming a species, define what the timber actually has to do: carry structural load, resist moisture or ground contact, take daily wear as furniture, or simply look good in a low-stress decorative role. Those are different demands with different answers, and starting from the job rather than a preferred species avoids the common mistake of choosing an attractive wood that is simply not suited to the use.

## Structural and load-bearing work

For beams, roof structure, decking or anything bearing load or exposed to weather and ground contact, durability and strength matter more than appearance. [Azobe](species:azobe) is a standard choice here specifically because of its density and natural resistance to decay and insect attack — properties that matter far more on a roof beam than grain figure does. Structural work also demands a higher, more consistent grade: straight grain, minimal knots, no splits that compromise strength. This is not the place to accept a discounted piece with visible defects because it is cheaper — a defect that is merely unsightly in a decorative piece can be a genuine safety issue in a structural one.

## Doors, window frames and semi-structural joinery

This middle category needs durability — doors and frames are exposed to handling, weather at the threshold, and years of use — combined with enough workability to cut clean joints. [Iroko](species:iroko) is a common choice precisely because it sits in this middle ground: durable and dimensionally stable enough for exterior exposure, workable enough for the joinery a door or frame requires.

## Furniture: balance weight, workability and appearance

For furniture, the calculation shifts toward workability, weight and appearance, with durability still relevant but less extreme than for structural work. [Ayous](species:ayous) suits furniture where lighter weight, ease of shaping and lower cost matter — cabinetry, panelling, everyday pieces. [Sapele](species:sapele) suits furniture where durability and a richer appearance matter more, and where the extra weight and cost are worth it for a piece meant to last and to look a particular way.

## Decorative and accent pieces

Where a piece exists primarily to look distinctive — an accent table, a decorative panel — appearance can lead the choice more than it would elsewhere. [Padouk](species:padouk)'s vivid colour is the clearest example of a species chosen substantially for how it looks rather than for structural or general-purpose properties. This is the one category where prioritising appearance over the practical factors that dominate elsewhere is a reasonable, deliberate choice rather than a mistake.

## Grade matters as much as species

Within any species, the grade of an individual piece — how straight it is, how many knots or defects it has, how consistently it was sawn and dried — affects its suitability for a job as much as the species itself does. A high grade of an ordinary species will often outperform a poor-grade piece of an expensive one for a given job. Inspect the actual piece in front of you, not just the label on the species.

## Ask your carpenter or supplier to explain their recommendation

A supplier or carpenter with real experience will usually have opinions about what suits your job, and a good one can explain those opinions in terms of the demands above — load, exposure, workability, appearance — rather than simply asserting that one species is "better." That explanation is itself a useful signal about who you are dealing with, in the same way covered in [How to verify your furniture](insights:how-to-verify-your-furniture-cameroon).
