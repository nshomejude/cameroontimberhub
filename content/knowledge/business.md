There is a difference between doing a timber deal and running a timber business,
and it is the difference between surviving one bad shipment and not.

A single purchase is a sequence of decisions with a beginning and an end, and the
[Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) walks that sequence — specification,
qualification, quotation, Incoterms, payment, inspection. **This page does not
repeat it.** This page is about the layer above: the currency your money is
actually exposed to, what a price is made of, where the working capital in this
trade is trapped, how a deal gets secured when the two parties are on different
continents, and which contract terms decide what happens when something goes
wrong.

It is written for both ends of the trade — the Cameroonian exporter deciding
whether a contract is worth taking, and the overseas importer deciding how much
of their balance sheet to expose to a counterparty they have never stood next to.

**No figures appear on this page.** No margins, no deposit percentages, no
financing costs, no fee levels, no exchange rates. Those are set by your species,
your volume, your counterparty, your bank and the week you are trading in. A
number invented here would be used to build a costing, and the costing would be
wrong.

## Three currencies, and only one of them is yours

The first thing that makes this trade specific rather than generic is that a
Cameroonian export sale almost always spans at least two currencies and often
three, and the party who does not think about it absorbs the difference.

**Costs at origin are incurred in CFA francs.** Cameroon uses the Central African
CFA franc, currency code XAF, issued by the Bank of Central African States (BEAC)
and shared across the CEMAC member states. Wages, stumpage, fuel, road haulage to
the coast, handling and local charges are all XAF costs, and they move with the
Cameroonian cost base rather than with a buyer's home market.

**The XAF is pegged to the euro at a fixed parity.** This is genuinely important
and is frequently missed by people who assume all African currencies float. A
euro-denominated contract from a Cameroonian exporter's point of view is, in
currency terms, close to a domestic-currency contract: the exchange exposure on
the sale itself is small, because the parity is fixed. That is a structural
advantage of this origin, and it is worth understanding before you decide what to
invoice in.

**A dollar-denominated contract is a different animal.** Much international
commodity trade quotes in US dollars, and a Cameroonian exporter selling in USD
against an XAF cost base is running a real, unhedged currency position between
the day the price is agreed and the day the money converts. Freight is frequently
quoted in dollars too, which can cut either way. If you quote in a currency other
than euro, know that you have taken a position, decide deliberately whether you
want it, and price the validity period of your quote accordingly — an offer left
open indefinitely in a foreign currency is a free option written in favour of the
buyer.

Two practical rules follow. **State the currency in every quotation, contract and
invoice**, next to the Incoterm and named place — a bare number is not a price.
And **state how long the quote is valid**, because a currency position and a
freight market both age.

Moving the proceeds is its own subject. Export receipts and foreign-currency
transfers within CEMAC are subject to exchange regulations administered through
BEAC and the commercial banks, and those rules have been revised and tightened
over time. We are not going to state what they currently require, because it is
the kind of detail that moves and that a page like this gets wrong. Ask your bank,
in advance, what documentation an inbound export payment needs and how long it
takes to be made available — and ask before you contract, not when the money is
sitting in suspense.

## What a price is actually made of

Most disputes that look like price disputes are scope disputes. Two quotes for
the same species differ because they contain different things, and neither party
has written down which things.

An exporter's cost build-up runs roughly: the standing material or the purchased
logs; transport to the mill; conversion, with a recovery rate that is a
commercial fact of the species and the log quality rather than a rounding error;
drying, where the material is dried, which takes time and time is money;
grading, sorting and rejection; treatment where the destination requires it;
bundling and marking; road haulage to Douala or Kribi; handling, documentation
and forwarding at the port; the export formalities; and then — depending on the
Incoterm — ocean freight and insurance. Overhead, finance cost and margin sit on
top of all of it.

Three consequences are worth stating plainly.

**Recovery is where the money is.** In a converted product, the difference
between a good and a poor yield from the same logs dwarfs most of the line items
below it. An operator who does not measure conversion recovery by species and by
log grade does not know their cost, and will win the contracts they should have
lost.

**The Incoterm moves the boundary of the price, not the cost.** The costs beyond
the named place do not vanish when you sell FOB; they are simply the buyer's. A
CIF price that was built by adding a guess to an FOB cost base is a loss already
agreed. Price the term you actually quoted. Both the
[Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) and the [Export Academy](/knowledge/export)
set out what each term does and does not include.

**Volume is measured, and the measure is a term.** State the unit — normally
[CBM](/knowledge/glossary) — and state the measurement convention and who
measures. Nominal against actual dimensions, gross against net, measured before
or after drying: each is a legitimate convention and each gives a different
number for the same stack of wood. Two parties using different conventions have a
dispute the day the invoice arrives.

## Working capital is the real constraint

The structural fact of this trade is that money goes out long before it comes in,
and the gap is measured in months, not days. Timber is bought or harvested, hauled,
converted, dried, stored, hauled again, loaded and shipped — and only then does a
payment mechanism release funds. Drying alone can hold a lot for a long time, and
a lot in a drying stack is capital that cannot be spent.

That is why undercapitalised exporters ask for large advances, and why buyers who
refuse every advance on principle end up dealing only with intermediaries who are
passing the problem to someone else. The commercial question is not whether
prepayment is acceptable — it is what the prepayment is secured by and what it is
tied to.

For an exporter, the discipline is to know your cash cycle by product: how long
from spending the first franc to receiving the last euro, per species and per
form. That number determines how many contracts you can run at once, which is a
harder ceiling than demand and is the one most growing operators hit first.

For a buyer, the equivalent discipline is to recognise that a supplier's cash
position is part of your delivery risk. A counterparty financing your order out
of your deposit while also financing two other orders out of two other deposits
is a fragile arrangement, and it fails in the order the deposits arrived.

## How deals get financed and secured

The mechanisms below are ordinary international trade instruments, and the
[Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) covers how they sit inside a single
transaction. Here the question is different: what they mean for a business that
intends to do this repeatedly.

**A documentary letter of credit** converts a buyer's promise into a bank's
undertaking. For an exporter it is the strongest commonly available assurance of
payment short of cash in advance, and — because the credit is a bank commitment
against documents — it can also be the basis on which the exporter's own bank is
willing to lend against the shipment. That second effect is the one that matters
strategically: a credit is not only security, it is often the thing that makes
pre-shipment finance possible at all.

The price of that is documentary discipline. Banks examine documents, not goods.
A presentation that does not conform is a discrepancy, and a discrepancy hands
control back to the buyer at precisely the moment the exporter has no cargo and
no money. An exporter who cannot reliably present clean documents will find that
credits cost them more than they are worth. Read the credit the day it arrives,
check that every term in it is one you can actually satisfy, and ask for
amendments while there is still time.

**Documentary collection** is lighter and cheaper and carries no bank
undertaking to pay. It suits established relationships and suits neither party in
a first deal with a stranger.

**Open account** — ship now, invoice, get paid later — is what mature
relationships converge on because it is cheapest and simplest. It is also
straightforwardly the exporter granting the buyer unsecured credit. Move to it
deliberately, after a payment history exists, and not because a buyer has asked
persistently.

**Staged payment against verifiable events** is the workhorse of this trade. The
principle that makes it work is to tie money to things that can be checked rather
than to dates: a satisfactory pre-shipment inspection report, a bill of lading, a
document set presented. A milestone nobody can verify is a date wearing a
costume.

**Credit insurance and guarantees** exist for both directions of this risk, and
so do export credit agencies in many importing countries. Whether any of them
will cover a given trade lane and counterparty is a question for a broker, not
for a web page. It is worth asking early, because the answer sometimes reshapes
how a deal is structured.

Two things that are not financing, and should never be treated as such. A
**proforma invoice** is an offer document, not a security. And a **certificate of
any kind** — including anything on this platform — is evidence, not a payment
guarantee. Cameroon Timber Hub does not hold funds, does not escrow, does not
inspect goods and does not stand behind any party's contract.

## Counterparty risk when nobody can drive to the other's yard

The defining feature of this trade is distance. The buyer cannot walk into the
mill, the exporter cannot visit the warehouse, and neither party's ordinary
instincts about who looks solid are getting any real information. Everything has
to be reconstructed from evidence.

**For the buyer.** Establish that the company exists as a legal entity, that the
people you are corresponding with belong to it, and that its bank account is in
its own name. Test its operational competence by asking for the complete document
set from a comparable past shipment — the single best diagnostic in this trade,
because assembling that file is exactly the work your order will require. Take
references from buyers in your own market and actually call them. Start small: a
first order sized so that its total loss would be survivable is the cheapest due
diligence available, and it buys information no amount of correspondence will.

**What the platform does and does not tell you.** Companies in our
[supplier directory](suppliers:) have been through an internal review — to appear
publicly a company must hold a live verification badge and a complete profile,
and badges, certificates and documents on a profile reflect documents reviewed by
Cameroon Timber Hub based on what the company submitted. That is a real filter
and it is worth using. It is not an audit of a mill, a credit assessment, or a
guarantee of performance on your order. Read a badge as a reason to shortlist,
never as a reason to relax.

**For the exporter.** Buyer risk is real and is usually underestimated at the
Cameroonian end, because the buyer is the one with the money and therefore feels
like the safe party. They are not. A buyer can refuse the cargo, dispute the
grade after arrival, delay while your container accrues charges at a port you
cannot reach, or simply fail. Check the company's registration and trading
history in its own country, ask which port and which customs broker they use,
and treat an order that is unusually large, unusually urgent and unusually
undemanding about specification as the warning it is.

**Fraud, in both directions, is mundane.** The recurring pattern in this trade is
payment diversion: an email, apparently from your counterparty, changing
previously agreed banking instructions, usually with a plausible reason and mild
urgency. It works because it looks routine. The defence is procedural, not
perceptive — **never act on changed bank details received by email; confirm by
voice on a number you already held before the change.** Make that a written rule
inside your own business, because the person who receives that email will not be
you.

## Contracts: which law, which language, which forum

A sale contract in this trade has to answer questions that a domestic contract
can leave to the background.

**Governing law and dispute forum.** Decide both, write both, and understand that
a judgment is only useful if it can be enforced where the other party's assets
are. Arbitration is common in international commodity trade largely because
arbitral awards travel across borders more readily than court judgments do. This
is a point on which to take actual legal advice rather than to copy a clause from
a previous contract.

**Cameroonian business law is OHADA law.** Cameroon is a member state of OHADA,
the Organisation for the Harmonisation of Business Law in Africa, whose Uniform
Acts govern commercial law, company forms, securities over assets and debt
recovery across the member states, with a common court of justice and
arbitration. For a foreign buyer this is genuinely useful to know, because it
means the commercial-law environment at origin is a harmonised regional
framework rather than something unique to one country — and because a
Cameroonian lawyer will frame advice in those terms. The detail is a matter for
counsel; the point here is simply to know which system you are in.

**Language.** Cameroon has both French and English as official languages, and the
timber trade operates substantially in French. That has practical consequences: a
contract may exist in two versions, official documents and correspondence with
the administration will often be in French, and a translation error in a
specification is a specification error. Where a contract is bilingual, **state
which language version prevails.** Where you are working across the language
boundary, put the botanical species name in every document, in every language, in
the same form — the [species directory](species:) records both botanical and
trade names, and species naming is the single most common documentary mismatch in
Cameroonian timber export.

**The terms that actually decide disputes** are not price. They are: the
specification and the grading rule it refers to; the measurement convention;
quantity tolerance and how the variance is priced; what inspection covers, who
pays for it and what a failure entitles you to; the complete document list with a
named party responsible for each item; delivery timing and what happens when it
slips; the notice period for claims after arrival, which is usually short and is
usually the reason a justified complaint fails; and force majeure. Write them.
A contract that is a price and a species is not a contract, it is an
introduction.

## Margin, and the discipline that protects it

A trading margin in this business is thin enough that the ordinary operating
failures eat it. Naming them is more useful than quoting a number nobody should
believe.

- **Demurrage and detention.** Time-based charges accrue daily on a container
  waiting for a document, and they do not care whose fault the delay is. They are
  the most common way a profitable shipment becomes an unprofitable one. The
  [Logistics Academy](/knowledge/logistics) covers the mechanics.
- **Rejected and downgraded material.** Every board that fails the grade was paid
  for at full cost. Grading discipline before shipment is margin protection, not
  quality theatre.
- **Claims after arrival.** Usually about moisture, staining, warp or grade —
  and almost always traceable to a decision made before the container was sealed.
- **Currency and quote ageing.** See above.
- **Freight moving between quotation and booking.** If you quote a delivered or
  CIF term, you have taken freight-market risk. Either fix the freight before you
  fix the price, or make the quote's validity short enough to matter.

The underlying discipline is per-lot costing. Businesses in this trade that
survive know what each shipment actually earned, by species and by form and by
customer, rather than what the year earned in total. The average conceals the
contracts that were losing money, and those are the ones a business tends to
repeat because the customer was pleasant and the volume was large.

## Building something repeatable

The economics of this trade reward repetition heavily. A first shipment with a
new counterparty carries the whole cost of qualification, the tightest payment
terms, the most inspection and the highest chance of a documentary problem. The
fifth carries almost none of that. Both sides therefore have a strong incentive
to convert a transaction into a relationship — which is a commercial strategy,
not sentiment.

For an exporter, the way to earn that is unglamorous and specific: consistent
grading against a stated rule, honest communication when something slips, a
document set that arrives complete without being chased, and the ability to
produce a legality file on request before anyone asks for a price. In markets
governed by destination-country legality and deforestation rules, the buyer
carries the liability — which makes an exporter who can evidence the chain a
commercially scarce asset rather than merely a compliant one. The
[Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance) is where that layer is set out.

For a buyer, it means treating a supplier who performs as an asset worth paying
promptly and forecasting for, rather than re-tendering every order to save a
margin that will be lost the first time a cheaper supplier ships the wrong grade.

If you are pricing a purchase, [post an RFQ](rfq:) so several suppliers quote one

is the whole point. If you are assessing who to trade with, the

and the certifications and documents each company holds, and the
