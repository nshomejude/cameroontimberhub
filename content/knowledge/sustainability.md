A sustainability claim is only as good as the evidence and the chain behind it.
A certificate is evidence. It is not proof, it is not a legal defence, and it is
not a substitute for knowing where your timber came from.

This is the page on this site with the strongest commercial incentive to
overclaim, so it is worth saying at the outset what it will not do. It will not
tell you that tropical hardwood is sustainable, or that it is not. It will not
print forest-cover figures, carbon numbers, deforestation rates or certified-area
statistics — no numbers appear on this page at all. It will not tell you what any
certification scheme guarantees in your case, because a certificate's meaning
depends on its scope, its standard, its version and what it actually covers,
which are things to read on the document rather than to learn from us. And it
does not claim that Cameroon Timber Hub verifies, audits or guarantees the
sustainability of anything sold through this site. We do not.

What it does is explain the mechanisms — what a certificate is, what a chain of
custody is, what makes a claim checkable and what makes it noise — so that you
can assess a specific claim from a specific supplier about a specific consignment.

## Three questions that get confused with each other

Almost every unproductive conversation about sustainable timber is caused by
sliding between these.

**Was it legal?** A documentary question about compliance with the law of the
country of production and with the rules of the market the goods are placed on.
It has a determinate answer and a file behind it. It belongs to the
[Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance), and everything about EUDR, CITES,
FLEGT and Cameroonian forestry law lives there, not here.

**Was it well managed?** A forestry question about how the forest is being
handled over time — what is cut, on what cycle, under what plan, with what effect
on the residual stand and on the people who depend on it. Legality is a floor
here, not an answer: a legally issued title says the operator was entitled to
harvest, not that the harvest was good silviculture.

**Can any of it be shown?** A traceability question. Whatever is true of the
forest, the buyer receives a container. What connects the two is a chain of
records, and a claim that cannot be traced back through that chain is an opinion.

**Legality is not sustainability, and certification is not legality.** That second
one matters most: voluntary schemes do not replace legal obligations. Whatever
certificate travels with the goods, the duty a regulation places on you stays
with you. The Compliance hub is explicit about this and this page will not
contradict it.

## What certification actually is

Third-party forest certification schemes exist — FSC and PEFC are the two most
widely referenced internationally — and they work broadly the same way. A scheme
publishes standards; independent certification bodies assess an organisation
against those standards; an organisation that passes holds a certificate with a
number, a scope, an issuing body and an expiry. Buyers use the certificate as
evidence about the organisation's practices.

Then there is a second, separate kind of certificate: **chain of custody**. The
[glossary](/knowledge/glossary) defines chain of custody as the documented,
traceable path of timber from harvest through processing and export to the final
buyer, used to verify legal origin. A chain-of-custody certificate is about the
organisation's systems for keeping certified material identified and separated
from uncertified material as it moves and is processed. It is *not* a statement
about a particular container unless a claim for that container is made on the
shipping documents, correctly, by an organisation whose certificate covers it.

Five things to hold on to, none of which is a criticism of the schemes:

- **A certificate has a scope.** It covers named sites, named activities and named
  product categories. A company certificate held for one mill and one product line
  does not automatically extend to a different mill, a different species or a
  different product. Read the scope on the certificate rather than the logo on the
  email.
- **A certificate has a validity period, and it can be suspended.** Certificates
  expire and status changes. Schemes publish public databases where a certificate
  code can be looked up, and looking it up is the single most useful thing a buyer
  can do with a certificate number.
- **The claim on the invoice is the operative thing.** For certified material, the
  claim travels on the sales documents for that consignment. A supplier holding a
  certificate and a consignment carrying a claim are two different facts.
- **Certification is voluntary, and its absence is not evidence of wrongdoing.**
  Certification is expensive and administratively heavy, and much legally
  harvested, well-documented timber is uncertified — particularly from smaller
  operators. Treating "no certificate" as "not legal" is both unfair and
  analytically wrong.
- **Schemes are systems for producing assurance, not guarantees of an outcome.**
  They are audited, they have complaints procedures, and they are also contested —
  by NGOs, by researchers and sometimes by each other. Reasonable people disagree
  about how much weight a given certificate should carry. What is not reasonable is
  treating it as the end of the enquiry.

We are not going to summarise any scheme's standards, categories, claim types or
rules on this page. They are published, they are versioned, they are revised, and
a paraphrase you relied on would be worse than the source you did not read.

## The honest tensions

Tropical hardwood sourcing involves real trade-offs, and a page that pretended
otherwise would not be worth reading.

**Standing forest competes with other land uses.** Forest that has no commercial
value is not automatically safe; in many landscapes the realistic alternatives to
managed timber production are agricultural conversion or unmanaged extraction, not
untouched wilderness. That is an argument for well-managed production, and it is
also an argument people make to excuse badly managed production. Both are true at
once, and the difference between them is exactly the evidence this page is about.

**Timber is a renewable material with a long clock.** A hardwood tree that reaches
export dimensions took a long time to do it, and the cutting cycle a forest is
managed on is measured in decades. That makes management plans and their actual
implementation, rather than a single harvest, the thing that matters — and it
makes short-horizon assurances hard to credit.

**Livelihoods are part of the system, not a footnote.** Forestry is employment,
tax revenue and local infrastructure in producing regions, and the communities
living in and around production forests have rights and interests that a
purely-environmental framing tends to lose. A sourcing policy that simply exits an
origin does not leave the forest better off by itself; it moves the buyer's
exposure, which is not the same thing.

**Substitution is not automatically an improvement.** Replacing a tropical
hardwood with another material carries its own footprint, and replacing a durable
species with a less durable one that has to be replaced sooner may cost more over
a building's life. Whether a substitution is better is a question about the whole
service life, not the purchase.

**Demand for lesser-known species can be a good thing or a bad one.** Spreading
harvest across more species reduces pressure on the few that everyone asks for by
name — and it also opens new markets for species whose management is less well
understood. Which effect dominates depends on the management behind it.

None of this resolves into a slogan. It resolves into asking better questions of a
specific supplier about a specific forest.

## What makes a claim checkable

A useful claim has four properties. A marketing claim usually has none of them.

1. **It names an entity.** Which company, which mill, which concession or title,
   which certificate number. "Our timber is sustainably sourced" names nothing.
2. **It is documented.** There is a certificate, a management plan, a title, a
   record — an actual artefact with an issuer, a date and a scope, not a paragraph
   on a website.
3. **It is verifiable independently of the seller.** A certificate code can be
   checked in the scheme's public database. A title number can be checked against
   the issuing authority. A claim whose only source is the person making it is not
   evidence; it is a statement of intent.
4. **It connects to your consignment.** The chain from the forest to your container
   is unbroken and reconstructable — harvest records, transport, mill, export
   documents, lot by lot. The mill is where most chains break, because that is
   where material from many sources is mixed.

Anything with fewer than four of these is worth exactly what it costs to say.

## The vocabulary to distrust

Not because everyone using it is dishonest — most are not — but because these
phrases carry no checkable content on their own, and it is worth noticing when a
supplier's assurance consists entirely of them.

- "Eco-friendly", "green", "responsibly sourced" with nothing attached.
- "Sustainable" as an adjective applied to a company rather than to a documented
  practice.
- "Certified" without naming the scheme, the certificate number and the scope.
- "FSC-controlled", "certified-source", "certification pending" and similar
  half-claims — these may correspond to a real, specific and legitimate status, or
  may be a paraphrase somebody chose. Ask which precise status is meant and ask to
  see the document.
- "Carbon neutral" applied to a shipment, without a stated methodology, boundary
  and instrument.
- "Legal and sustainable" as a single compound phrase. They are two claims with
  two different kinds of evidence, and joining them lets the stronger one carry the
  weaker.

Being precise about this matters more here than anywhere else on the site.

Companies in our [supplier directory](suppliers:) have been through an internal

Read all of that for what it is: **a record of what a company has stated and
submitted, reviewed as a document.** It is a filter worth using and a reasonable
place to start a shortlist.

It is **not** an audit of a forest, a mill or a management plan. We do not visit
concessions, we do not inspect goods, we do not assess forest management, and we
do not verify or guarantee that any timber sold through this site is sustainably
produced. A badge or a certification line on a listing is a starting point for
your own verification — check the certificate in the scheme's own database, check
the title with the issuing authority, and build your own file. Where we have not
assessed something, the page says so; and forest management is something we have
not assessed for anyone.

## For a buyer

1. **Settle the legal question first**, because it is the one with an enforceable
   answer and, in several markets, a liability attached to you personally. Which
   market you are placing goods on determines the regime. Go to the
   [Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance) — EUDR and CITES in particular are
   covered there, and neither is answered by a sustainability certificate.
2. **Ask for the certificate itself, not the logo.** Number, scope, issuing body,
   validity. Then look it up in the scheme's public database yourself.
3. **Ask whether the claim covers your consignment**, and how it will appear on the
   shipping documents.
4. **Ask for the chain**, lot by lot, from harvest title to export document. A
   supplier who can assemble that file for a past shipment within a few days can
   assemble it for yours. One who cannot never will.
5. **Do not treat uncertified as disqualified.** Ask instead what documentation the
   supplier does hold and how their material is traced. Many good operators are
   uncertified.
6. **Write it into the contract.** If certified material is what you are buying,
   name the scheme, the claim, the documents required and the remedy if they do not
   arrive — the [Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) covers how to make a requirement
   enforceable rather than aspirational.
7. **Keep your own records.** Under the regimes that matter, an exporter's
   assurance is not a defence.
8. **Do not repeat a claim you cannot evidence.** Whatever a supplier told you
   becomes your claim the moment you put it in front of your own customer, and the
   consumer-protection rules on environmental claims in your market are yours to
   answer to.

## For a Cameroonian supplier

Sustainability documentation is increasingly the difference between an enquiry and
an order, and the operators who benefit are the ones who can produce evidence
rather than adjectives.

- **Get the traceable file right before you get the certificate.** Harvest title,
  plot data, transport records, mill records and export documents, linked lot by
  lot, are what every serious buyer asks for — certified or not — and they are the
  foundation any certification would sit on anyway.
- **Fix the mill.** It is where chains of custody break, because it is where
  material from different sources meets. Whatever your system for keeping lots
  identified through processing, be able to describe it in writing.
- **State certificates precisely.** Scheme, number, scope, validity. Precision
  reads as competence; a vague claim reads as a risk, and an unverifiable claim is
  worse for you than an honest gap — a buyer who checks and finds a mismatch is
  gone permanently.
- **Do not use scheme names or logos loosely.** Schemes have rules about their own
  trademarks and claims, and buyers in regulated markets are increasingly careful
  about repeating claims they cannot substantiate.
- **Say what you do not have.** An operator who says "we are not certified, here is
  our documented chain, here is our management plan" is credible. One who implies
  certification and cannot produce a number is not.
- **Keep the evidence current.** Certificates expire, titles have terms, and a
  document that was true last year is not automatically true now.

## About this hub

The Sustainability Academy currently holds **no articles**. This pillar page is the
whole of the hub so far, and it is deliberately a page about how to assess a claim
rather than a page making claims.

There are no numbers on it. No forest-cover figures, no deforestation rates, no
carbon numbers, no certified areas, no proportions of anything — not because they
do not exist, but because publishing them here would put a marketplace's name
behind statistics we have not produced, cannot maintain and have a commercial
interest in choosing favourably. Nor does this page state what any certification
scheme's standards require or what any certificate guarantees, because those are
published, versioned documents that should be read rather than paraphrased by an
interested party.

We have not assessed the forest management of any supplier on this site, and we do
not verify or guarantee sustainability claims. What we can tell you is what a
company has stated and submitted, and what a claim would need to look like for you
to be able to check it yourself. Where we have not assessed something, the page
says so — and on this subject, that is most of it.
