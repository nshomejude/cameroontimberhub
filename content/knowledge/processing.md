A tree is not a product. Somewhere between a stem lying at a forest roadside and
a bundle strapped in a container, a series of decisions gets made — how to open
the log, how dry to take the boards, what to machine, what to treat, what to sell
and what to burn — and those decisions set the price, the quality and most of the
things that later go wrong.

This page is about that middle. It is written for two readers at once: the
Cameroonian operator deciding what capacity is worth having, and the buyer trying
to work out from a phone call whether the mill on the other end actually knows
what it is doing. Both are asking the same question from opposite ends — what has
already been done to this wood, how well, and who carries the consequences.

Three neighbouring pages carry loads this one does not. The
[Timber Fundamentals](/knowledge/fundamentals) hub covers the **material
science** — why wood shrinks, what moisture content means, how density and
durability behave — and this page assumes it rather than repeating it. The
[Products Academy](/knowledge/products) covers **what each traded form is** and
how it is specified. The [Export Academy](/knowledge/export) picks up at the
point the goods are finished: documents, customs, ports and getting a container
onto a vessel. This page is what happens in between, and it is the part of the
chain where the most value is created and the most is quietly lost.

One thing you will not find here: numbers. No recovery percentages, no kiln
schedules, no drying times, no moisture targets, no treatment retentions, no
standard designations. Every one of those is set by species, by log quality, by
equipment, by product and by contract, and a figure invented on a content page
would be acted on by somebody. Where a number matters, this page says what it
depends on and who to get it from.

## Processing is a decision about where risk sits

There is a single idea underneath everything below, and it is the same one the
[Products Academy](/knowledge/products) states from the buying side: **every
processing step someone performs is a step somebody else does not have to
perform, and a risk somebody else stops carrying.**

A green board sold at the mill gate is cheap because the buyer has bought the
drying risk along with the wood. A kiln-dried, planed, graded, packaged board
costs more because the mill has absorbed the losses that occur between those two
states — the degrade, the trim, the pieces that came out of the kiln unusable —
and has priced them in.

So when two quotations differ, the useful question is rarely "why is one dearer?"
It is "what has one of them already done, and what does the other one leave for
me?" A buyer with kilns, a moulder and a yard can genuinely profit from buying
further back. A buyer without them is buying a problem, and the discount is the
size of the problem.

Three things move together along that line:

- **Where quality is determined.** Every processing step either preserves the
  quality the log had or destroys some of it. It cannot add any. A superb kiln
  cannot fix a badly sawn board and a superb saw cannot fix a rotten log.
- **Where the losses land.** Volume disappears at every step. Whoever performs
  the step pays for the volume that disappears during it.
- **Where the evidence lives.** Claims about drying, treatment and grade are made
  by the party who did the work. If they did not record it, nobody can verify it
  afterwards.

## Conversion: opening the log

Conversion — breaking a log down into boards — is the step with the largest
irreversible consequences, because everything downstream is constrained by the
pieces it produces.

### The sawing decision

Every log presents the same trade-off: the sawyer can cut for **volume**, for
**width**, for **grade**, or for **stability**, and cannot maximise all four.
Cutting to chase the clear outer wood produces high-grade narrow boards and a
low-grade core; cutting straight through produces the widest boards the log can
give and accepts whatever the centre turns out to be, which is exactly why
[boules](/knowledge/glossary) exist as a traded form. Cutting to a customer's
fixed section list produces the pieces that customer wants and a pile of
offcuts nobody ordered.

How the board sits relative to the growth rings also decides how it will move.
That is material behaviour, and the [Timber Fundamentals](/knowledge/fundamentals)
hub explains why: wood moves far more in one across-grain direction than the
other, so two boards from the same log, cut at different angles, will cup and
shrink differently for the rest of their lives. The commercial consequence is
that stability and appearance are partly a sawing decision, they cost yield to
obtain, and a buyer who needs them has to say so and pay for them. A
specification that asks for dimensional stability without saying anything about
how the material is to be cut has asked for something it has not defined.

### What the log lets you do

A mill's options are bounded before the saw starts. Diameter caps the widest
board available. Length, sweep and taper decide how much has to be trimmed off
before anything square can be produced. Internal defect — heart shake, rot,
brittleheart, ingrown bark, insect galleries, and in some species mineral
inclusions hard enough to damage saw teeth — is invisible from outside and
becomes the sawyer's problem at the first cut.

Elapsed time matters too, and it is the most under-discussed variable in
Cameroonian processing. A felled tropical log in a tropical climate starts
degrading immediately: ends split, sapwood stains, borers arrive. Time from
felling to conversion is a quality parameter, and "how long had the logs been
down?" is a legitimate question for a buyer to ask about the parcel being
offered.

### Green, resawn, and remanufactured

Not all conversion happens once. Material is commonly broken down green, dried in
a rough oversize, and brought to final section afterwards — because drying
distorts, and a board that was perfectly dimensioned before it dried is not
perfectly dimensioned after. The practical consequence for a buyer is that
**"sawn to size" and "sawn oversize and machined to size after drying" are
different products** with different tolerances, and only one of them is reliably
within tolerance on arrival. It is worth asking which you are being quoted.

## Recovery and yield: the number nobody can honestly give you

This is the commercial heart of processing, and the place where most
misunderstanding between buyer and mill lives.

**Recovery** is how much sawn volume a mill gets out of a given volume of log.
**Yield** is how much of what you bought you can actually use for the thing you
are making. They are different numbers, held by different people, and neither is
a property of the species.

We publish no recovery figure for any species, and you should be suspicious of
anyone who publishes one as a general fact. It varies with log diameter,
straightness and defect; with the sawing pattern chosen; with saw kerf and how
well the machine is maintained; with the product being cut and how tight its
tolerances are; with the operator; and with what the mill can sell its offcuts
and low grades for. Two mills cutting the same species from the same forest to
different product lists will not report the same number, and neither of them is
lying.

What is stable enough to state, and useful:

- **Sawdust and offcuts are not waste, they are cost.** Every millimetre of kerf
  is log converted into nothing. Every trim to remove sweep, split or defect is
  volume paid for and not sold. This is why a log is cheap per cubic metre and a
  finished component is not: the difference is largely the volume that stopped
  existing along the way.
- **Tight specifications cost recovery.** Fixed lengths cost more than random
  lengths. Wide boards cost more than narrow. Heartwood-only costs more than
  heartwood-and-sapwood. Clear costs more than knotty. Each of those is a
  requirement that forces the sawyer to reject material that was physically
  sound. That is where the price difference comes from — not from the mill being
  greedy.
- **Your yield is yours to measure.** A parcel that grades correctly can still
  yield badly against your cutting list — a defect that is a minor downgrade in a
  wide board can write off a short billet entirely. Nobody in Cameroon can
  predict your yield from a distance. If yield matters commercially, buy a trial
  parcel and measure it before you contract for volume.
- **Grade recovery is a separate question from volume recovery.** A mill can
  produce a great deal of sawn timber out of a log and very little of it in the
  grade you asked for. When a supplier says a species "cuts well", ask what they
  mean: volume, or grade.

For an operator, the same arithmetic runs the other way. The route to a better
margin is rarely a bigger machine. It is usually **finding a home for the volume
that is currently falling on the floor** — shorts, narrows, off-grade and
offcuts. A mill with one product line converts everything that does not fit that
line into loss. A mill with a secondary outlet — components, short-length
mouldings, [squares and scantlings](marketplace:squares), edge-glued panels,
[charcoal and biomass](marketplace:charcoal) from residues — sells material that
was already paid for at the log yard.

## Drying as an operation

The [Timber Fundamentals](/knowledge/fundamentals) hub covers what drying *is*
and why it matters — hygroscopicity, shrinkage, the difference between free and
bound water, why the target should be set by the destination environment and not
by the yard. This section covers drying as something a business actually has to
do: with space, time, energy, capital and a schedule.

The distinction matters because the science and the operation fail in different
ways. The science says a board dried too fast will check. The operation is where
"too fast" becomes a decision made by someone under pressure to ship on Friday.

### Air drying is a use of land and time

[Air drying](/knowledge/glossary) is cheap to run and expensive in every other
resource. It needs covered, ventilated space; correctly sized and aligned
stickers so air moves between every board; weight or restraint on top of the
stack so the boards on top do not distort; and it needs the stack to sit there
for as long as the timber requires rather than as long as the yard can spare.

Three operational realities follow:

- **Working capital is tied up in the stack.** Timber that is drying is money
  that has been spent and cannot be invoiced. This is the single strongest
  commercial pressure toward shipping material wetter than it should be, and it
  is why a buyer's moisture specification has to be a contractual line with a
  consequence, not a preference.
- **Stacking discipline is most of the outcome.** Badly aligned stickers, sticker
  spacing too wide for the thickness, stickers of uneven thickness, a stack not
  square, or stickers wet enough to stain the boards they touch — these are the
  ordinary causes of a parcel that arrives distorted or marked, and none of them
  is exotic.
- **Air drying has a floor set by the climate.** It cannot take timber below the
  equilibrium that the surrounding air supports, and in humid coastal Cameroon
  that floor is well above what a heated interior in a temperate market demands.
  A supplier who offers air-dried material for a European joinery specification
  is not necessarily being dishonest; they may simply be offering the wrong
  process for the requirement. That is a conversation to have before the order,
  not after.

### Kiln drying is capacity, and capacity is finite

[Kiln drying](/knowledge/glossary) buys what air drying cannot deliver: a target
below the local equilibrium, in a controlled time, with the ability to relieve
the stresses drying sets up. What it costs is capital, energy, skilled operation
and — the constraint people forget — **a chamber that can only hold one schedule
at a time.**

That last point drives more real-world behaviour than anything else. A kiln is a
batch process. Thick stock and dense species need long, gentle schedules; thin
stock and light species need short ones. Mixing species or thicknesses in a
charge means one of them is being dried on the wrong schedule. So when a mill is
running a mixed order book against a full kiln and a vessel date, the temptations
are all in the same direction: mix the charge, shorten the run, or ship the
parcel a little wetter than it should be. Every one of those decisions is
invisible on a certificate and visible in your workshop months later.

For an operator, the honest framing is that **kiln capacity is what lets you sell
into specification-driven markets at all.** Buyers who need a stated moisture
content are, in practice, buying kiln access. That is a capital decision, not a
sales one.

### What actually goes wrong

Drying defects are mostly the same problem — the outside of a board dried faster
than the inside could keep up with:

- **Surface checking**, where the outer fibres shrink against a wet core that
  will not let them.
- **End splitting**, because ends lose moisture far faster than faces. End
  sealing exists for exactly this reason and is not optional on valuable stock.
- **Case hardening**, where the piece appears sound and is carrying locked-in
  stress. It looks fine until it is resawn or machined, at which point it moves
  or pinches the blade. This is the defect that most often surprises a buyer,
  because it passes visual inspection perfectly. Kiln operators relieve it
  deliberately as part of the schedule — which is a step that can be skipped, and
  is skipped when the kiln is needed for the next charge.
- **Internal checking**, which is invisible entirely until the board is cut.
- **Collapse, distortion and staining**, from over-aggressive drying, poor
  stacking or timber left too long in conditions that favour fungi.

Notice what these have in common: **most drying defects are cheap to prevent, and
none of them are repairable.** A kiln cannot un-check a board. This is why drying
is the step where the gap between a competent mill and an ambitious one shows up
most clearly.

### Making a drying claim mean something

"Kiln dried" is a process, not a moisture content. For a claim to be worth
anything, four things have to be true, and a buyer can ask about all four:

1. **A target and a tolerance were specified** — and whether that is an average
   across the parcel or a maximum for any piece. An average conceals wet boards,
   and it is the wet boards that cause the problem.
2. **It was measured**, with a meter, on a stated number of pieces, at depth
   rather than at the surface on thick stock. Ask for the readings themselves,
   not a summary.
3. **It was measured close to loading.** Drying is reversible. A parcel that came
   out of the kiln correctly and then sat uncovered in a humid yard for weeks is
   no longer at the figure on the certificate.
4. **It was protected afterwards.** Wrapping, covered storage, and how the
   container was loaded all decide whether the number survives the voyage. That
   part is covered in
   [loading a container of sawn timber](insights:loading-a-container-of-sawn-timber).

A supplier who can answer those four without hesitation is telling you something
real about their operation. One who answers "yes, kiln dried" and stops has told
you nothing.

## Machining and secondary processing

Beyond sawing and drying sits everything that turns a board into a component:
planing and thicknessing, ripping and cross-cutting to size, moulding to a
profile, sanding, finger-jointing, edge-gluing into panels, and assembly.

The rules worth carrying:

- **Machine dry, or machine twice.** Wood moves as it dries. Anything machined to
  a tolerance before it has reached its service moisture content will be out of
  tolerance later. This is the reason for the rough-oversize-then-finish sequence
  described above, and the reason a mill without kilns cannot credibly sell tight
  tolerances.
- **Machining quality is species-dependent as much as machine-dependent.**
  Interlocked grain, which is common in tropical hardwoods, tears out on planing
  unless the tooling is sharp and the cut is taken correctly. Dense species blunt
  cutters fast. A profile that runs beautifully in one species can be a fight in
  another, and no specification can tell you this in advance — which is why
  [mouldings](marketplace:mouldings) should be bought against samples run on the
  actual profile in the actual species.
- **Consumables and maintenance are a real cost line.** Tooling for dense
  tropical hardwood is consumed quickly, and a mill running blunt cutters
  produces fuzzy grain and burnish marks that only appear once a finish is
  applied. This is a normal thing to ask about in a factory visit.
- **Finger-jointing and laminating are how short and narrow material is
  rescued.** They turn offcuts into saleable length and width, which is exactly
  the recovery argument above. They also introduce a glue line, and a glue line
  is a specification: the adhesive has to suit the exposure the finished piece
  will see, and the pieces have to be at the right moisture content when they are
  glued. [Laminated and edge-glued panels](marketplace:laminated_panels) fail at
  the joint almost every time, and almost always for one of those two reasons.
- **Packaging is a processing step.** Bundling, strapping, wrapping and marking
  determine whether the quality you created survives to the buyer, and they get
  decided last by people who are tired. See the
  [Export Academy](/knowledge/export) for how that connects to the document set.

Sorting and grading also happen here, and they are their own discipline: what a
grade guarantees, under which rule, and how to specify it is covered by the
[Quality & Grading Academy](/knowledge/grading).

## Preservative treatment

Treatment is where two entirely different things get called by the same word, and
the confusion is expensive.

**Treatment for durability** is applied because the timber will face an exposure
its own natural durability cannot survive — ground contact, persistent wetting,
insect pressure. It is a product property, it is specified by the buyer's market,
and it belongs in the contract.

**Treatment required by plant-health rules** is applied because a destination
country's border requires it for the consignment to be admitted. It is a
regulatory requirement, it is set by the destination, and it is covered in the
[Export Academy](/knowledge/export). Do not assume one satisfies the other. And
note the separate point that page makes: the rules that apply to solid wood
packaging used inside a container are not the same as the position of the cargo
itself.

For the durability kind, the mechanisms worth understanding:

- **Penetration is the whole game.** A preservative protects the wood it actually
  reaches. Surface application protects a surface; anything that cuts, drills or
  planes through it exposes untreated wood underneath. This is why treated
  components should be cut to final size before treatment wherever possible, and
  why field cuts on treated material are a genuine weak point.
- **Heartwood resists treatment; sapwood accepts it.** This is the fact that
  makes "durable species" and "treated timber" different answers rather than
  interchangeable ones. In many species the heartwood simply will not take
  preservative to any depth. A durable species does not need it; a non-durable
  one is mostly treatable in the part that was least durable to begin with.
- **The timber has to be dry enough first.** Wet wood has no room for a
  preservative to go. Treatment is a downstream step, and a mill without drying
  capacity has limited treatment options regardless of what plant it owns.
- **A treatment claim is a claim against a standard.** Which preservative, to
  what penetration and retention, verified how, under whose rule. Those
  parameters come from the standard that applies in the buyer's market — not from
  the supplier, and not from this page. **We publish no treatment standards,
  chemicals, penetrations or retentions.** Put the requirement in the enquiry and
  ask what the supplier can evidence.

This matters most for the forms that go into ground contact or exposed service:
[poles and posts](marketplace:poles), [sleepers](marketplace:sleepers),
[decking](marketplace:decking) and structural [beams](marketplace:beams). For
those, species identity and durability are doing the work that a treatment claim
would otherwise have to do, and both are worth verifying.

## Processing closer to source

Whether timber should be processed further before it leaves Cameroon is both a
policy question and a commercial one. This page takes no position on the policy —
and deliberately makes no statement about what Cameroonian rules currently
require or permit for any product form. The
[Export Academy](/knowledge/export) explains why we hold that line, and the
answer for your species and your product form has to come from MINFOF and your
own clearing agent.

The commercial mechanics, though, are stable and worth setting out plainly,
because they explain why the argument exists at all.

**What processing at origin gains.** Value that would otherwise be captured
elsewhere stays in the country. Weight and volume drop — drying alone removes
water that would otherwise be shipped across an ocean at freight rates paid per
container, and trimming removes material that was going to be discarded anyway.
Because dense tropical hardwood hits a container's payload limit long before it
fills the box, sending drier, more finished material means a container carries
more sellable product and less water and waste. Employment and skill accumulate
where the work is done, and residues have a local use.

**What processing at origin costs.** Capital, in kilns, machines and buildings.
Reliable power, which is not a trivial assumption. Maintenance and spare parts,
in a market where lead times on both can be long. Skilled operators and grading
staff, who have to be trained and kept. And it concentrates risk: material
processed to a specific buyer's fixed cutting list is worth much less if that
buyer walks away, whereas a general commodity board has many possible homes. Each
step forward narrows the market for the piece.

**What it changes for the buyer.** Buying more processed material at origin means
buying more of the outcome and less of the raw input — which is good if the
supplier is capable and worse if they are not, because quality decisions you
would have made yourself are now being made by someone you cannot watch. It also
moves the point where a defect becomes discoverable further from you. That is
precisely why the qualification and pre-shipment inspection steps in the
[Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) get more important, not less, the more
processed the product is.

There is no general answer. There is only a specific one for a specific mill,
species, product and buyer.

## Judging a mill from the outside

If you are a buyer who cannot visit, these questions separate an operator from an
intermediary. None of them require a number in reply — they require a shape.

- **What do you actually own and operate?** Which conversion equipment, how many
  kiln chambers, what machining plant. A supplier who buys in processing is fine,
  but you should know it, because it changes who controls the quality and how
  quickly problems get fixed.
- **What is the sequence for my order?** Green sawn to size, or oversize then
  machined after drying? The answer tells you whether the tolerance you asked for
  is realistic.
- **How do you dry, to what target, and how do you measure it?** Then: is that an
  average or a maximum, and can I see the readings?
- **What happens to the material that does not make my specification?** A mill
  with an answer has a business. A mill with no answer is either absorbing that
  loss into your price or is about to send you the material anyway.
- **How is my lot segregated, from log yard to bundle?** This is a quality
  question and a legality question at the same time. Traceability breaks at the
  mill more often than anywhere else in this trade — see the
  [Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance).
- **What do you record, and can I see it for a past order?** Drying records,
  grading records, treatment records, loading photographs. A supplier who can
  produce a file for an old shipment quickly can produce one for yours.

For the operator reading the same list: those are the questions the good buyers
ask, and the ability to answer them is worth more commercially than another
machine.

## About this hub

The Processing Academy currently holds **no articles**. This pillar page is the
whole of the hub so far, and it is deliberately a page about mechanisms rather
than a manual: it explains why a thing happens so that you can reason about your
own mill, your own species and your own order, and it stops at the exact point
where a real number would have to be invented.

That is why there are no recovery or yield percentages on this page, no kiln
schedules or drying times, no moisture targets, no treatment chemicals,
retentions or penetrations, and no standard designations anywhere in it. Recovery
in particular is the figure people most want published and the one it would be
most irresponsible to publish: it varies by species, log quality, sawing pattern,
product and mill, and a buyer or an investor acting on a general figure would be
acting on fiction. The honest answer is that you get it by asking several mills
the same written question, or by cutting a trial parcel and measuring it.

Articles will appear beneath this page as they are written and checked. We are
not going to list what they will be, because a promised article is not a
published one. Where we have not assessed something, the page says so.
