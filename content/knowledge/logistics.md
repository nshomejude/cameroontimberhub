Most of the money lost between a Cameroonian mill and an overseas warehouse is
not lost to theft, damage or bad wood. It is lost to waiting — a container that
sat because a document was late, an empty that was returned after the free period
ran out, a transhipment that missed its connection, a consignment that arrived
before the paperwork that releases it. None of that is dramatic, and almost all
of it is avoidable by someone who understood the sequence.

This page is about that layer: the actors, the bookings, the deadlines and the
liabilities that sit between a finished consignment and a delivered one. It is
written for the exporter who is arranging carriage for the first time and for the
buyer who wants to know what their supplier's forwarder is actually doing on
their behalf.

**What this page deliberately does not cover.** The
[Export Academy](/knowledge/export) covers the Cameroon-side export operation —
the document set, phytosanitary requirements, Douala and Kribi, customs, and
where consignments fail — and this page assumes it. It also covers loading, and
the article
[loading a container of sawn timber](insights:loading-a-container-of-sawn-timber)
goes properly deep on stowage, moisture, weight distribution, dunnage and
securing. **We are not going to re-explain how to stuff a container here;** read
that. And the [Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) covers the purchase itself, with
[Incoterms for timber buyers](insights:incoterms-for-timber-buyers) setting out
EXW, FOB, CFR and CIF in detail.

Nor will you find numbers. No transit times, no freight rates, no free-time
periods, no demurrage or detention charges, no container capacities. Freight
pricing moves constantly, free time is set per carrier, per trade and per
contract, and a figure invented here would be used to build a costing. Where a
number matters, this page says what it depends on and who to ask.

## Who is actually who

The most common cause of confusion in a first shipment is that the exporter does
not know how many separate companies are involved or what each one is liable for.
Roles overlap in practice, and one company frequently wears several hats — which
is fine, provided you know which hat it is wearing on your shipment.

**The carrier** is the shipping line: it owns or operates the vessel, it issues
the bill of lading, and it is the party contractually responsible for the
carriage. Its liability for the goods is defined by the terms of that bill of
lading and by the international carriage conventions that apply — and it is
limited. This is the single most important thing to understand about carriers:
**a carrier is not an insurer.** Their liability is capped, it is subject to
defences, and it is nowhere near the value of a container of tropical hardwood.
That gap is what cargo insurance exists to fill.

**The freight forwarder** arranges carriage. They are, in the ordinary case, an
agent: they book space, coordinate the moving parts, prepare or collect
documents, and advise. A good forwarder is the single most valuable relationship
an exporter has, because they hold the calendar the whole shipment runs on.

**An NVOCC** — a non-vessel-operating common carrier — is a forwarder who has
gone further and taken on the role of carrier itself. It contracts with you as
the carrier, issues its own bill of lading (a "house" bill), and separately
contracts with the actual shipping line, which issues a "master" bill to the
NVOCC. This is a normal and legitimate structure. It matters for two reasons.
First, **your contract is with the NVOCC, not the line** — so if the NVOCC fails,
you have a problem with a party you may never have dealt with. Second, **some
banks and some letters of credit will not accept a house bill of lading.** If
payment runs through a documentary credit, check what the credit requires before
the booking is made, not after the document is issued.

**The clearing agent** — the customs broker — handles the export declaration and
customs formalities in Cameroon. The [Export Academy](/knowledge/export) makes
the point that agents are not uniformly effective at both ports, and that a good
agent at the right port is worth more than a paper advantage at the wrong one.

**The terminal and the port authority** handle the physical container: receipt,
storage, loading and discharge. They are not your counterparty, but their
deadlines are, because the terminal's gate-in cut-off is a real wall.

**At the destination**, the mirror image: the buyer's own customs broker, a
delivery agent, sometimes the same forwarder's overseas office, and a haulier for
the final leg.

The practical rule that follows: **write down, per shipment, who each of these is
and who is instructing them.** Under an FOB sale the buyer nominates the carrier
and the exporter must work with a forwarder chosen by someone else; under CFR or
CIF the exporter chooses. Confusion about who is instructing whom is how
containers end up unbooked.

## How a booking actually works

A booking is not one action. It is a sequence with several independent deadlines,
and the deadlines are what people miss.

1. **You request space** with the line or through your forwarder, for a named
   port of loading, a named port of discharge, a vessel and voyage, an equipment
   type and a number of units. At this point you need to know your cargo well
   enough to state it: commodity, weight, and whether the consignment is a full
   container load or a part load.
2. **The booking is confirmed**, and you receive a booking reference and the
   cut-off dates. Those cut-offs are the spine of everything that follows.
3. **An empty container is released** to you or to a nominated depot, and you
   collect it. Equipment availability is a real constraint at origin, not a
   formality — an empty has to physically be in the country before it can be
   stuffed.
4. **You stuff and seal**, capturing the container and seal numbers. See
   [loading a container of sawn timber](insights:loading-a-container-of-sawn-timber).
5. **You submit shipping instructions** — the data the bill of lading will be
   drawn from — before the documentation cut-off. This is the moment the
   description of goods, the marks, the piece counts and the consignee details
   become hard to change.
6. **You declare the verified gross mass** of the packed container. The shipper
   is required to declare it before the box is loaded on board, and a container
   without a declared mass does not get loaded. Confirm with your line how they
   want it obtained and submitted, and by when.
7. **The container is gated in** at the terminal before the terminal cut-off, and
   customs clearance is completed.
8. **The vessel sails**, and the bill of lading is issued against the goods as
   actually shipped.

Three things worth internalising about that list.

**The cut-offs are staggered and they are not the sailing date.** Documentation
closes before the box has to be at the terminal, which closes before the vessel
sails. An exporter working backwards from the sailing date will miss two
deadlines they did not know existed. Ask for all of the cut-offs in writing at
the moment the booking is confirmed.

**A confirmed booking is not a guaranteed sailing.** Space gets rolled — the box
is not loaded and waits for the next vessel — for reasons that include vessel
capacity, weight limits, port congestion and the line's own priorities. It
happens, it is normal, and it is why a contract that promises an arrival date
based on a single booking is a promise you do not control. Say "estimated" and
mean it.

**Full-container and part-container shipments behave differently.** A full
container load is your box, sealed by you, opened by your buyer. A part load is
consolidated with other people's cargo at origin and deconsolidated at
destination, which adds two handling operations, two more places for the goods to
be damaged or misrouted, and an extra set of charges at the far end that
first-time buyers routinely do not expect. Timber's weight makes the arithmetic
awkward: a small parcel of dense hardwood can be heavy enough that consolidation
is unattractive, while a full container of it can reach a payload limit before it
is full. Whether to consolidate is a question for your forwarder with your actual
weights in front of them.

Logs, and other cargo that will not go in a box, move as breakbulk on vessels
that carry it, which is a different booking process with different equipment,
different loading arrangements and a much thinner set of available services from
any given port. If that is your trade, the routing question dominates everything
else, and it starts with which vessels call.

## The bill of lading, and why the paper moves at its own speed

The [Export Academy](/knowledge/export) explains what each export document is
for. The logistics question is a different one: **when does each document exist,
where is it physically, and what is waiting for it?**

The bill of lading is the one that governs the answer. It is issued after
shipment, and in its negotiable form it is a document of title — the goods are
released at destination to whoever presents it properly. That creates the
structural problem of this trade: **the vessel and the paper travel separately,
and either can arrive first.**

The consequences are practical.

- **If the documents arrive after the vessel**, the consignee cannot take
  delivery. The container sits, and it accrues charges from the moment the free
  period ends — while everyone waits for a courier or a bank. This is one of the
  most common and most avoidable costs in the whole chain.
- **If a documentary credit is in play, the bank is in the path.** Documents go
  to a bank, are examined, and move onward only if they conform. A discrepancy
  adds days at exactly the point where days are expensive. Read the credit the
  day it arrives and get it amended while there is still time — the
  [Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) covers the payment mechanics.
- **Originals are physical objects that can be lost.** A lost negotiable bill of
  lading is a serious problem, not an administrative one, and the remedies are
  slow and expensive. Courier them properly, track them, and split the set across
  more than one envelope where the practice allows.
- **There are alternatives, and they are choices with consequences.** A
  non-negotiable form, or an electronic or "telex" release arrangement, removes
  the race between vessel and paper — but it also removes the control that
  holding the original set gives the seller, because the goods can then be
  released without it. That is a trade between a logistics risk and a payment
  risk, and it is a decision for the seller with the payment terms in front of
  them, not a default. Agree it before the booking, and make sure it is
  compatible with whatever the payment mechanism requires.

The general rule: **the document plan and the shipping plan are one plan.** Agree
the full document set, who produces each item and how it will physically travel,
before the container is stuffed.

## Transhipment: your cargo probably changes vessels

Very few routings are a single vessel from a Cameroonian port to a final
destination port. Cargo is commonly carried to a hub, discharged, and reloaded
onto a second vessel — sometimes more than once. This is ordinary and not a
defect in the service, but it changes the risk profile and it is worth knowing
before you quote a delivery expectation.

What transhipment actually costs you:

- **Every transfer is a handling operation**, and handling is where containers
  are dropped, scraped and mishandled. A well-secured stow matters more on a
  transhipped routing than a direct one, because the box gets moved more.
- **A missed connection is a wait**, and it does not have to be anybody's fault.
  The onward vessel's schedule, hub congestion and capacity all decide it.
- **Tracking gets harder** at exactly the moment you most want visibility. The
  cargo can be sitting at a hub for a while with no obvious event against it.
- **Direct services, where they exist, cost more and are worth it more often
  than people expect** on high-value consignments with a real deadline.

The question to put to your forwarder is not "how long does it take?" It is
"**what is the routing, is it direct or transhipped, where does it tranship, and
how frequent is the onward service?**" That answer tells you what the realistic
range is and where the schedule is likely to break — which is far more useful
than a single number that will not survive contact with a hub.

Which port you sail from is bound up with this. As the
[Export Academy](/knowledge/export) puts it, a port is only useful to you if a
vessel is going where your cargo needs to go, and that consideration dominates
every other. The routing decision and the port decision are the same decision.

## Arrival, clearance and the last mile

Most exporters stop thinking at the moment the vessel sails, and most first-time
buyers have not thought about arrival at all. That is where a surprising share of
the cost lives.

On arrival the container is discharged, and then it waits for the consignee to
clear it. Import clearance is the buyer's operation in almost every Incoterm that
is normally quoted from Cameroon — the seller's responsibility ends earlier — and
it requires the destination country's own document requirements to have been
satisfied. That includes the plant-health requirements the
[Export Academy](/knowledge/export) covers and the legality file the
[Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance) covers, both of which are set by the
destination and therefore have to be known before the goods left.

Then there are the charges nobody quoted. A CFR or CIF price gets the goods to
the destination port and no further, and destination terminal handling, customs
clearance fees, any inspection ordered by the authorities, storage, and inland
haulage to the buyer's premises all sit outside it. A buyer comparing an FOB
quote with a CIF quote and forgetting this is comparing two numbers that do not
mean the same thing. Ask your forwarder for the **landed** cost, itemised, and
ask specifically what is not included.

Finally: **inspect and record on arrival, immediately.** Notice periods for
claims against a carrier and against a seller are short and are in the documents
you already signed. Photograph the seal before it is broken, the doors when they
are opened, the stow before it is disturbed, and any damage in place. A claim
raised after the timber has been unloaded, moved and stacked is very hard to
sustain even when it is entirely justified.

## Demurrage and detention

These two words describe the most predictable avoidable cost in container
shipping, they are routinely confused, and they are usually charged per container
per day, which means they compound quietly while everyone is discussing whose
fault it is.

**Demurrage** is what you pay when a container sits inside the terminal for
longer than the free period allows — typically a container that has been
discharged and not yet collected. **Detention** is what you pay when you have the
container outside the terminal for longer than allowed — typically an empty that
was collected for stuffing, or a full box collected at destination, and not
returned in time. One is the box occupying the port's space; the other is the box
occupying the carrier's asset.

Both apply at origin and at destination, and both are governed by a "free time"
allowance set by the carrier and negotiable in a way most exporters do not
realise. **We publish no free-time periods and no charge rates**: they vary by
carrier, by trade, by port, by equipment and by what your forwarder negotiated,
and they change. Get them in writing for your specific booking, at both ends,
before you accept it.

What actually causes them, in rough order of frequency:

- **Documents arriving after the vessel.** See above. This is the big one.
- **Import clearance not ready** — a missing plant-health or legality document, a
  query from customs, an inspection ordered.
- **The consignee is not ready to receive**, has no haulage arranged, or has no
  space.
- **Payment not completed**, so the release is withheld.
- **At origin**, an empty collected too early and stuffed too late, which is a
  self-inflicted detention charge and an easy one to plan away.

Three things reduce the exposure genuinely:

1. **Send document copies immediately and originals early.** Scanned copies let
   the consignee's broker begin work; the originals only have to arrive before
   release.
2. **Agree in the contract who bears these charges and in which circumstances.**
   The Incoterm allocates the main costs but does not resolve an argument about a
   delay neither party planned. Say it explicitly.
3. **Know your free time at both ends before you book**, and treat it as a
   deadline you are managing rather than a number in a tariff you will read
   later.

## Cargo insurance

The single most important sentence in this section: **the carrier's liability is
not insurance, and the Incoterm rarely gives you the cover you think it does.**

Two facts sit behind that. First, a carrier's liability for loss or damage is
limited by the terms of carriage and the applicable conventions, and it is
subject to defences — perils of the sea, insufficient packing, inherent vice of
the goods. A carrier successfully arguing that a load of timber arrived stained
because it was loaded too wet is arguing inherent vice or bad packing, and it is
frequently a good argument. Second, most Incoterms simply do not oblige anybody
to insure. Under FOB and CFR, nobody is required to buy cover; risk passes to the
buyer at shipment and the goods travel uninsured unless the buyer arranges it.

CIF is the exception, and it is a trap in its own way: it obliges the seller to
buy insurance, but the obligation is for **minimum** cover. As the
[Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) and the
[Incoterms article](insights:incoterms-for-timber-buyers) both note, if you need
broader cover you must say so in the contract or arrange your own.

What a buyer or seller should actually establish, in writing, for every shipment:

- **Who is insuring**, and from which point to which point. The gap you are
  looking for is the moment risk passes under the Incoterm — cover must be
  continuous across it, and it must include the inland legs at both ends, not
  only the sea passage.
- **On what basis** — the breadth of cover, and what is excluded. Insufficiency
  of packing, inherent vice and pre-existing condition are standard exclusions,
  and they are exactly the categories a timber claim tends to fall into.
- **For what value**, and on what terms the sum insured is calculated. Cover for
  the invoice value alone leaves the freight and the duty uncovered.
- **Who claims, and against what evidence.** Photographs, survey reports, timely
  notice, and the original documents. Insurers decline claims for late notice and
  thin evidence far more often than for anything exotic.

Timber's specific vulnerabilities are worth naming to whoever is writing the
cover: moisture and condensation damage in a sealed box, staining and mould,
distortion in transit, and shifting of a stow. Note that these are also the
categories most likely to be excluded as packing or inherent-vice problems —
which is another way of saying that **insurance is not a substitute for loading
the container properly.** It covers what you could not prevent, not what you
chose not to.

## Where the Incoterm bites

The Incoterm is not a shipping arrangement, and treating it as one causes most of
the arguments in this section. It divides three things: who pays which costs,
where risk of loss passes, and who handles which formalities. It says nothing
about when the goods arrive, what happens if they do not, or who is at fault for
a delay.

Both neighbouring pages cover the terms themselves in full — the
[Export Academy](/knowledge/export) from the exporter's side, the
[Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying) and
[Incoterms for timber buyers](insights:incoterms-for-timber-buyers) from the
buyer's. The logistics-specific points worth adding here:

- **Cost and risk are two lines, and under CFR and CIF they are in different
  places.** The seller pays the carriage to destination; risk passed at shipment.
  A CIF seller watching a delayed vessel is watching the buyer's risk.
- **Always name the place.** "FOB" is not a term. "FOB Douala" and "FOB Kribi"
  are different contracts with different inland costs.
- **The term does not allocate demurrage and detention cleanly**, particularly
  for a delay caused by neither party. Write it down.
- **The term does not require anyone to insure** except CIF and CIP, and then
  only minimally.
- **A term that looks convenient can be unrealistic.** A delivered term into a
  market whose inland costs and clearance practice you have not priced is a loss
  you have already agreed to.

## What actually goes wrong

Nearly every expensive logistics failure in this trade is one of six things:

1. **The documents were later than the ship.** Charges accrue while nothing
   happens.
2. **A cut-off was missed** because nobody asked for the cut-off dates when the
   booking was confirmed.
3. **The declared weight was wrong or late**, and the box did not load.
4. **The paperwork did not match the cargo.** Piece counts, volume, weight, marks,
   container and seal numbers appear across several documents and are checked
   against each other. Any divergence invites inspection, and inspection costs
   time even when it ends in your favour.
5. **Nobody owned a task.** The exporter assumed the forwarder was doing it, the
   forwarder assumed the clearing agent was, and the buyer assumed the exporter
   was.
6. **The goods were uninsured, or insured for the wrong leg**, and a carrier
   claim recovered a fraction of the loss.

None of those is a shipping problem. They are all coordination problems, which is
why this hub exists as something separate from the export procedure itself.

## Questions worth asking a forwarder

Before you appoint one, and before every unfamiliar routing:

- **Which lines do you work with out of Douala and Kribi to my destination, and
  is the service direct or transhipped?**
- **Give me every cut-off for this booking in writing** — documentation, verified
  gross mass, terminal gate-in.
- **Will the bill of lading be a house bill or the line's own?** And if a
  documentary credit is involved: does the credit accept what you are going to
  issue?
- **What free time do I have at origin and at destination, and what happens if I
  exceed it?**
- **What is the landed cost, itemised — and what is not in that number?**
- **Who is my contact at destination, and what does the consignee need to have
  ready before arrival?**
- **What insurance is in place, from what point to what point, on what basis, and
  what is excluded?**

For a buyer qualifying a Cameroonian supplier, the equivalent test is simple:
ask who their forwarder is, at which port, and ask to see the document set from a
comparable past shipment. Specific, consistent, fast answers are the signal.
Reassurance is the absence of one.

You will not find transit times, freight rates, free-time allowances, demurrage
or detention charges, container capacities or liability limits anywhere on this
page. Free time and rates are negotiated per carrier and per booking and change
constantly; liability limits depend on which terms and conventions apply to your
particular carriage. Publishing any of them as a general fact would give a reader
a number to build a costing on, and the costing would be wrong. Get them in
writing, for your booking, from the party that is bound by them.
