If you have decided to buy tropical hardwood and Cameroon is one of the origins
on your list, this page is the orientation: what kind of place the timber comes
from, how it physically gets from a forest to a ship, which institution sits at
the centre of it, and what practically changes for you as a buyer because the
origin is this one rather than another.

It is also written to be useful to a Cameroonian operator explaining their own
country to a first-time overseas customer, because that conversation happens on
every new account and it goes better when both sides are working from the same
picture.

## Where Cameroon sits

Cameroon is a Central African country with an Atlantic coastline. Much of its
southern interior lies within the **Congo Basin**, the great tropical moist
forest region of central Africa, and it is that forest — not a plantation
resource — that the export timber trade draws on. The country's geography changes
markedly from south to north; the timber trade is a southern and coastal
business, and the north of the country is not part of this story.

Two features of the country's position matter commercially. First, it has its own
maritime coast, so its timber reaches the sea inside its own borders rather than
crossing a neighbour's — the export operation is described in the
[Export Academy](/knowledge/export). Second, it lies within a Central African
economic and monetary community, sharing a currency and a regional commercial-law
framework with its neighbours; the commercial consequences of that are covered in
the [Business Academy](/knowledge/business).

## The producing regions, as our own data shows them

Rather than assert a map, we can tell you what the platform's own species
catalogue records. Every entry in the [timber species directory](species:) carries
the Cameroonian regions in which that species is recorded as available, and
across the whole catalogue those records fall into five regions: the **East**,
the **South**, the **Centre**, the **Littoral** and the **South-West**. That is
the working footprint of the Cameroonian timber trade as this platform holds it.

Read across the catalogue and a shape emerges that is worth knowing before you
enquire.

**Some species are recorded across essentially the whole forest zone.**
[Ayous](species:ayous) and [Iroko](species:iroko) are both recorded in all five
regions, which is part of why they are the names an overseas buyer meets first:
availability is broad, and supply is not dependent on one corner of the country.

**Many of the classic commercial hardwoods cluster inland.**
[Sapele](species:sapele), [Sipo](species:sipo), [Kosipo](species:kosipo),
[Tiama](species:tiama), [Padouk](species:padouk), [Doussie](species:doussie),
[Okan](species:okan) and [Bubinga](species:bubinga) are all recorded in the East,
South and Centre — the interior forest. Some are recorded narrowly:
[Moabi](species:moabi), [Wenge](species:wenge) and
[Assamela](species:assamela) sit in the East and South only.

**Some species are coastal or south-western.** [Okoume](species:okoume) is
recorded in the South and Littoral, [Niangon](species:niangon) and
[Zingana](species:zingana) in the South-West and Littoral, and
[Abura](species:abura) in the Littoral, South-West and South.
[Azobe](species:azobe) — the heavy, durable species used where water and ground
contact are involved — is recorded across the South-West, Littoral, South and
East.

Why this is more than trivia: **where a species grows determines how far it has
to travel to reach a port, and inland haulage is a real part of the cost and of
the calendar.** An interior species is not worse or more expensive by rule, but
its supply chain is longer, and that is a fair thing to ask a supplier about. It
also means that "who can supply this species" is a narrower question than "who
exports timber", which is why the [supplier directory](suppliers:) lets you filter
by species handled and by region.

A caution on all of the above: regional availability describes where a species
occurs and is traded, not what any particular company has in stock this month, and
not what may lawfully be harvested from where. Both of those are questions for the
supplier and for the administration respectively.

## What actually grows here

Cameroon's forests carry a wide range of commercial hardwoods, and the trade
sorts them less by botany than by role.

**The high-volume export names.** Light, stable, easily worked timbers led by
[Ayous](species:ayous) — traded internationally as Obeche — which goes into
mouldings, joinery blanks and plywood core.

**The classic reddish-brown furniture and joinery hardwoods.** The mahogany-family
timbers — [Sapele](species:sapele), [Sipo](species:sipo),
[Kosipo](species:kosipo), [Tiama](species:tiama), [Acajou](species:acajou),
[Bosse](species:bosse) — traded for appearance, workability and consistency.

**The heavy, naturally durable structural timbers.**
[Azobe](species:azobe), [Tali](species:tali), [Okan](species:okan),
[Bilinga](species:bilinga) and [Doussie](species:doussie), used where durability
and density are the point: marine and hydraulic work, heavy construction,
exterior decking and joinery.

**Decorative and specialty timbers.** [Bubinga](species:bubinga),
[Wenge](species:wenge), [Zingana](species:zingana), [Padouk](species:padouk) and
[Ebene](species:ebene), traded on figure and colour rather than on volume — and
the group where species-level trade controls are most likely to be in play.

**Lesser-known species pushed to broaden the harvest.** A large part of the
catalogue consists of timbers that are technically capable but commercially
unfamiliar — [Dabema](species:dabema), [Kotibe](species:kotibe),
[Eyong](species:eyong), [Lati](species:lati), [Faro](species:faro),
[Alep](species:alep) among many others. For a buyer willing to qualify a
substitute on its properties rather than its name, this is where availability is
easiest.

Two things that group is *not*: it is not a statement of what may be exported in
what form, and it is not a statement of any species' regulatory status. Species-
level controls under CITES, and the destination-market legality regimes, are set
out in the [Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance). Each entry in the
[species directory](species:) records the CITES position we hold for that
species and shows a gap as a gap rather than filling it with a guess. The
technical properties that decide whether a species suits your job are covered in
[Timber Fundamentals](/knowledge/fundamentals).

## From forest to ship

The physical chain is short to describe and is where most of the risk in a
Cameroonian purchase actually lives.

1. **Harvesting**, under a title or permit issued by the administration, in a
   forest area to which conditions attach.
2. **Extraction to a forest landing**, and measurement and marking of the logs.
3. **Road haulage** from the interior to a mill or to the coast.
4. **Conversion at a sawmill** — into [sawn timber](marketplace:sawn_timber),
   [boules](marketplace:boules) or further-processed forms — with drying,
   treatment and grading as required. What happens here is the
   [Processing Academy](/knowledge/processing)'s subject.
5. **Bundling, marking and haulage to the port.**
6. **Export formalities, stuffing and shipment.**

Two observations about this chain that matter more than the sequence itself.

**The mill is where traceability breaks.** Almost every chain-of-custody failure
in this trade happens at the same point: material from several sources is
processed and stacked together, and afterwards nobody can say which board came
from which title. Whether a supplier segregates and records lot by lot is the
single most informative question you can ask them, and the answer predicts far
more than their price does. See [chain of custody](/knowledge/glossary) and the
[Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance).

**Road haulage is a real constraint, and it is seasonal.** The forest zone has
marked wet and dry periods, and the state of the roads between the interior and
the coast changes with them. This affects when material can be moved, and
therefore when it can be shipped. We are not going to publish a production
calendar — it varies by region, by route and by year — but it is entirely
reasonable to ask a supplier how their own production and haulage calendar works
and what it means for your delivery window. A supplier who answers that question
specifically is telling you something real about their operation.

## Ports

Cameroon's timber exports move through two deep-water gateways on the Atlantic
coast: **Douala**, the long-established main port through which most Cameroonian
export trade has historically run, and **Kribi**, the newer deep-water port to
the south.

We publish no comparative figures for either — no throughput, no draught, no
dwell times, no handling costs. The [Export Academy](/knowledge/export) sets out
what the choice between them actually turns on, which is a set of questions for
your forwarder rather than a table. The one thing to carry away here is
commercial: **"FOB" is not a term. "FOB Douala" and "FOB Kribi" are different
contracts**, and a quotation that leaves the port unstated is a dispute that has
not surfaced yet.

## Institutions, and how far we will describe them

The forestry administration is the **Ministry of Forestry and Wildlife
(MINFOF)**. It administers the system of forest titles and permits under which
harvesting takes place, and the forestry-side authorisations that an export
requires. Cameroon also operates a computerised forestry information system,
referred to in the trade as **SIGIF II**, used in registering operators, titles
and volumes.

Beyond that, this page stops, and the reason is worth stating rather than hiding.
The categories of forest title and permit, the rules for how they are allocated,
their names and their current procedure have changed over time and are reported
inconsistently in secondary sources — including sources that sound authoritative.
Writing them down here as settled fact would produce a page that reads well and
misleads an operator who acts on it. So:

- **For what a title or permit is, and what it currently permits:** MINFOF and
  the current official texts, through a Cameroonian professional.
- **For what a destination market obliges you to hold and evidence:** the
  [Compliance Academy](/knowledge/compliance), which maps the layers — origin
  legality, destination-market deforestation and legality law, CITES, and the
  voluntary schemes on top — and which is equally explicit about the points it
  will not resolve. It takes no position on the current status of Cameroon's
  Voluntary Partnership Agreement with the EU, and neither does this page.
- **For the export operation itself:** the [Export Academy](/knowledge/export).

The same restraint applies to a question both buyers and exporters ask
constantly: what may leave Cameroon, in what form. The rules on the export of
unprocessed timber and the timelines around them have shifted and are reported
inconsistently, so this site does not state them anywhere — every entry in the
[species directory](species:) records log export status as *Unknown*, because we
could not verify it to a standard we were willing to publish. **Verify the
current position for your species and product form with MINFOF and your clearing
agent before you contract.** If a counterparty tells you what the rule is, ask
them which instrument it comes from.

There is a general principle underneath all of this. An operator ID, a permit
number or a registration written on a letterhead is a claim, not proof. Anything
commercially material should be checked against the body that issued it.

## What being a Cameroonian-origin buyer actually changes

If you are used to buying from another origin, these are the practical
differences worth internalising.

**Language.** French and English are both official languages in Cameroon, and the
timber trade operates substantially in French. Documentation and dealings with
the administration will frequently be in French, correspondence may cross the
language boundary, and a bilingual contract needs to say which version prevails.
The operational consequence is specific: **fix the botanical species name in the
contract and carry it, in the same form, across every document.** Species naming
is the most common documentary mismatch in this trade, and a language boundary
makes it more likely, not less. The [species directory](species:) records
botanical, trade and local names for each species we have assessed.

**Trade names travel badly.** Many Cameroonian species are known internationally
under a different name — Ayous is Obeche, and it is not the only one. Buy on the
botanical name and the technical specification, not on the name you recognise.

**Counterparty types differ.** The export trade contains sawmillers, forest
title holders, trading companies and brokers, and they are not interchangeable.
A trader can be an excellent counterparty; the point is to know which you are
dealing with, because it determines who controls the quality and who controls the
documents. Qualification is covered in the
[Buyer Academy](/knowledge/buying).

**Legality evidence is the commercial differentiator here.** Because the
destination market normally places the legality burden on the importer, an
exporter who can produce a complete, linked file on request is commercially
scarce — and one who cannot is a risk you carry, not one they carry.

**Currency.** Costs at origin are in Central African CFA francs, and the currency
of your contract determines who is exposed to what. The
[Business Academy](/knowledge/business) covers it.

Work from the material outwards. The [species directory](species:) records the

Unfamiliar terms are defined in the
[Cameroon timber glossary](/knowledge/glossary).

## About this hub

Cameroon Timber 101 currently holds **no articles**. This pillar page is the
whole of the hub so far, and it is deliberately the most restrained page on this
site, because it is the one where a confident error would do the most damage.

You will not find a single statistic here — no forest area, no volumes, no
economic or employment figures, no rankings, no counts. You will not find the
forest tenure and permit system described as settled fact, a statement of what
may be exported in what form, or a position on the status of Cameroon's
Voluntary Partnership Agreement with the EU. Those are not oversights and they
are not placeholders to be filled in later by guesswork; they are things we would
have to verify against primary sources before publishing, and until then the
honest page is the quieter one.

What is here is what we can stand behind: the geography in general terms, the
regional availability our own catalogue records species by species, the physical
chain from forest to ship, the name of the administration, and the practical
consequences for someone trading. Articles will appear beneath this page as they
are written and checked. We are not going to list what they will be, because a
promised article is not a published one. Where we have not assessed something,
the page says so.
